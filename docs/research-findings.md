# Fretboard Lab: Research Findings

_Researched 2026-11-13. Every source listed under Sources was opened and read. Claims we could not check are marked **unverified**._

## Summary

- **Swap Levels 2 and 3.** The theory courses we could check (musictheory.net, Berklee Music Theory 101) teach the major scale before intervals. Teach whole and half steps in Level 1, build the major scale from them in Level 2, then teach intervals as scale degrees in Level 3. Keep Levels 4–6 in your order.
- **Don't make Level 1 "memorize the whole neck."** No course we checked does that. Teach the natural notes on the E and A strings plus octave shapes. After that, keep note-finding going in every level through spaced review.
- **Improvise from Level 2, not only in Level 6.** Berklee's beginner course has students improvising in lesson 4, and Hal Leonard does it in Book 2. Give each level a small improvising challenge, with Level 6 as the full version.
- **Use the learning-research principles that have solid evidence.**
  - Retrieval practice: make the learner tap or play the answer instead of re-reading.
  - Spaced review: bring back fact-like items (notes, intervals, chord spellings) over days.
  - Interleaving: once the basics are learned, mix drill types together.
  - Short daily sessions are sensible, but the evidence specific to music is thin.
- **Competitors leave a gap.** The guided apps (Yousician, Fender Play, Simply Guitar) are "very light on theory" and don't explain *why*. The theory apps (Tenuto, fretboard trainers) offer no guided path, and their drills don't carry over to real playing. Users also complain most about two things: microphone detection that blocks progress, and aggressive subscription billing.
- **Lessons without video work.** Ableton Learning Music, Hooktheory, Brilliant and musictheory.net show the pattern: one idea per card, the sound playing as the diagram highlights, the learner trying before the answer is shown, and a free-play sandbox at the end.

## 1. How courses order their lessons

| Course | Topic order (abbreviated) | What they teach first | Confidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Leavitt, *A Modern Method for Guitar* Vol. 1** (Berklee's core text) | Tuning, staff, note values → 1st-position notes → sharps and flats → chord-diagram accompaniment → keys G, F, Am, Em, Dm, B♭, D, A, E♭ in 1st position → movable chord forms → position playing, major-scale fingerings in 2nd–4th position | Reading notation and 1st-position notes | High (we read the table of contents) |
| **Berklee Online, Guitar for Beginners** | Basics and bass lines → power chords → pentatonic pattern 1 → **improvising** → pentatonic theory and ear training → open chords → strumming → chord and key theory → notation → tab | Power chords; pentatonic comes before open chords | High |
| **Berklee Online, Guitar Fundamentals** | Basics → reading → major and pentatonic scales plus improvising → open chords and strumming → common progressions → modes → movable triads → 7th and barre chords → blues → chord theory | Basics, then reading | High |
| **Berklee Online, Music Theory 101** | Rhythm → pitch notation → major scales → minor scales → **intervals** → triads → inversions → 7th chords → voice leading | Rhythm and notation | High |
| **Hal Leonard Guitar Method Bk 1–3** | Bk 1: 1st-position notes, C/G/G7/D7/Em chords, rhythms. Bk 2: more open chords, power chords, pentatonic and improvising, tab introduced. Bk 3: major, minor and pentatonic scales, barre chords, movable scales, chord construction | Notation, 1st-position notes, easy open chords | Medium (publisher descriptions, no table of contents) |
| **musictheory.net** | Staff and rhythm → major and minor scales, scale degrees, key signatures → **intervals** → triads and 7ths → diatonic chords and Roman numerals → progressions and cadences | Staff and note reading | High |
| **JustinGuitar** | **Unverified.** Every justinguitar.com page returned "access denied" | — | None |
| **Fender Play** | Riff- and song-based path, set by a sign-up survey on instrument and genre ("22 minutes to a beginner's first recognizable riff") | A first riff | Low (press release only) |

**Where they agree**
- **Scales and keys come before intervals** (musictheory.net, Music Theory 101).
- **Chords come after scales and intervals**, and **progressions come after chords**. This matches your Levels 4 and 5.
- **Improvising comes early**, tied to the pentatonic or major scale (Berklee lesson 4, Hal Leonard Book 2).

**Where they differ**
- **Note names on the neck.** The notation-based methods teach notes one position at a time. None has a "whole neck first" unit.
- **Chords first or scales first.** It varies: Hal Leonard starts with chords, Berklee Beginners with scales, and Leavitt teaches both together.
- **Notation or tab.** Leavitt and Hal Leonard start with notation. Berklee Beginners leaves notation and tab until the end.
- **CAGED.** None of the courses names it. The closest equivalents are "movable chord forms" and "movable triads", and they always come after open chords and scales.

## 2. What the research says about learning music

| Principle | What it is | Strength of evidence | How to use it in the app |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Retrieval practice** | Recalling an answer yourself rather than re-reading it | **Strong (non-music).** After one week, people who were tested recalled 61% and people who re-read recalled 40% (Roediger & Karpicke 2006). Dunlosky et al. 2013 rate it "high utility." | End every lesson with a quiz: "tap all the Cs," "name this interval by ear." Prefer tapping or playing the answer over multiple choice. |
| **Spaced repetition** | Reviewing the same item spread out over time | **Strong for facts, limited for motor skills.** A meta-analysis of 317 experiments on verbal recall supports it (Cepeda et al. 2006). One piano study found no effect, but its gaps were only minutes apart. | A review deck for fact-like items (note at a fret, interval shape, chord spelling, scale degree), with gaps that grow to days and weeks and a "due today" count. |
| **Deliberate practice** | Effortful, goal-directed work on specific weak spots, with feedback | **Moderate.** It explains about 21% of the variation in music performance (Macnamara et al. 2014). Another analysis of music-only studies finds a larger link, r = .61 (Platz et al. 2014). Researchers argue about the size, not whether it matters. | Track accuracy and speed for each item and build "weak spot" drills from them automatically. Let users set a goal for each session. Give feedback right away. |
| **Interleaved practice** | Mixing types of item or skill within a session | **Moderate in general, limited for music.** A study of 10 clarinetists leaned positive but most differences were not significant (Carter & Grahn 2016). The general meta-analysis is unverified. | Teach new material on its own first, then mix it in. Put easily confused pairs together (m3 vs M3, 4th vs 5th). Tell users that mixed practice feels harder but helps them retain more. |
| **Short daily sessions** | Short sessions spread over days instead of one long session | **Limited for music.** It's backed mainly by the general learning research. A keyboard study reportedly found gains only with a 24-hour gap, which suggests sleep matters. **Unverified:** only a search summary. | Default to a 10–15 minute daily session, with a streak or practice log, and suggest "come back tomorrow" instead of doing more today. |
| **Theory on the instrument** | Tying each concept to its sound and to the hand movement that plays it | **Limited and indirect.** Links between sound and movement form within minutes and grow stronger over weeks (Bangert & Altenmüller 2003; Lahav et al. 2007). No controlled study shows that theory taught on the fretboard beats theory taught on its own. | Every concept gets three parts: its position on the fretboard, its sound, and the learner playing it. Use "hear it → find it → play it" drills. Ear-training answers are taps on the fretboard, not text labels. |

**Caveats:** The strongest evidence comes from verbal and academic learning, so applying it to guitar is an extrapolation. The music-specific studies are small, with 9–29 participants. Describe the "theory on the instrument" approach as consistent with the research, not proven by it.

## 3. What users say about existing apps

> **No Reddit coverage.** Reddit blocks the fetch tool, and the search tool won't return Reddit results. This section draws on review sites, app-store reviews and two guitar forums instead.

| App | What people like | Most common complaints |
|---|---|---|
| **Yousician** | Accurate note detection, motivating game format, clear Level 0–10 path, slow-down and loop | Little theory and no "why"; its theory path stops at Level 6. Lessons start to "all feel very similar." The mic misses unamplified electric guitar. Pricing is expensive and confusing, with auto-renew. |
| **Fender Play** | Short, high-quality videos, clear beginner path, cheap | "Very light on theory," theory section hard to find, no feedback on your playing, shallow, billing problems |
| **Fretello** | Structured practice sessions, backing tracks | Hard paywall, basics not explained, doesn't adapt, repetitive. One user had to play a string 40 times to unlock the next lesson. |
| **Simply Guitar** | Engaging, real-time feedback, songs tied to concepts | Almost no theory. Detection rejects chords played correctly. Trials turn into a paid year. Shallow for intermediate players. |
| **Fretonomy** | Clean fretboard, many game modes, a heat map for each fret, wrong answers aren't punished | The fretboard is drawn differently from how players see their own guitar. Mix of subscription and one-time purchase. Users want sound added to the drills. |
| **Fret Pro** | Uses your real guitar and spaced review, one-time purchase | Misses roughly 1 in 5–10 notes and blocks progress. Progress bars go backwards. |
| **Tenuto** (musictheory.net) | Very customizable, strong ear training, $4.99 one-time | Overwhelming for beginners; bare fretboard drills |
| **JustinGuitar Note Trainer** | A forum user finally learned the notes after mnemonics failed | Forum replies said note names alone aren't enough; you need intervals, CAGED and triads too. |

**Recurring complaints**, roughly most common first:
1. Microphone detection rejects correct playing and blocks progress.
2. Paywalls, surprise trials and auto-renew.
3. Shallow theory with no "why", and content that runs out after the beginner stage.
4. Gamification gets repetitive and "rewards repetition, not skill."
5. Drills don't carry over to playing. Note names are taught without intervals or key, and the fretboard isn't drawn the way the player sees it.

**What people like:** a clear path in short chunks, instant feedback, visible progress (heat maps, statistics), a low-pressure game feel, and fair one-time pricing.

## 4. Teaching without video

| Example | Format | What makes it work |
|---|---|---|
| **Ableton Learning Music** | Short browser lessons, each a couple of sentences plus a widget | You learn by toggling things and hearing the result. It moves from ready-made patterns, to mixing them, to writing your own, and each chapter ends in a "play with" sandbox. |
| **musictheory.net** | 39 single-topic animated slide lessons | One narrow topic per lesson, building up to analysis of real pieces |
| **Hooktheory I** | Interactive e-book with 88 examples you can replay | Uses notation based on what a chord or note does in the key, so no sheet music is needed. Pop-song examples and a "Check For Understanding" at the end of each chapter. |
| **Brilliant** | Interactive, problem-based lessons | One concept per lesson, visuals first, instant feedback, and it tests you before teaching. |
| **Duolingo** | Lessons of a few minutes | Patterns start simple and are then applied in new contexts. Hints are optional and difficulty adapts. |
| **Chrome Music Lab** | Hands-on browser experiments | No account needed; you start playing right away. |
| **muted.io Guitar Scales** | Fretboard visualizer | Toggle between note names, intervals and scale degrees; highlight CAGED positions. No audio. |
| **Bret Victor, "Explorable Explanations"** | Essay | The interactive part belongs inside the explanation, and "the author must guide the reader." |

**Rules for a fretboard lesson card**, each based on Mayer's multimedia learning principles or on the examples above:
1. **One idea per card**, and the learner taps to move on. No autoplay.
2. **Play the sound the moment its dot lights up**, so arpeggios step through dot by dot in time.
3. **Put labels on the fretboard** ("R", "b3" inside the dot), not in a caption below.
4. **Remove everything that isn't the point of this card.** Grey out frets outside the scale and drop decorative wood textures.
5. **Use one color code everywhere.** The root is always the same color, and so is the 3rd.
6. **Keep text to one or two sentences.** Let the diagram and sound carry the idea, and don't repeat the same words in text and audio.
7. **Have the learner try before you reveal:** "Tap where you think the 5th is," then show and play it.
8. **Teach the vocabulary first.** Explain what R, 3 and 5 mean before using them.
9. **Guide first, then open a sandbox** at the end of each unit, linked to the free-play tab.
10. **Tie concepts to real songs** and keep lessons to a few minutes.

## 5. Recommendation

### The six levels

Changes from your draft: Levels 2 and 3 are swapped, Level 1 covers less of the neck, and every level includes some improvising.

| # | Level | Lesson topics | Playing challenge |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1** | **The neck and note names** | 1. String names and fret numbers (vocabulary card) · 2. The musical alphabet, and that there's no sharp between B–C or E–F · 3. Whole and half steps on one string · 4. Natural notes on the low E and A strings · 5. Octave shapes for finding any note on the other strings · 6. Sharps and flats · 7. Landmarks: the 5th, 7th and 12th frets | Play a note the app names (for example "G on the A string") within 3 seconds, ten times, using octave shapes to check yourself. |
| **2** | **The major scale and keys** _(was Level 3)_ | 1. The whole/half-step pattern W-W-H-W-W-W-H on one string · 2. One-octave major scale shape from the 6th and 5th strings · 3. Scale degrees 1–7 · 4. Moving the shape to any key · 5. Key signatures and the circle of fifths (light) · 6. The relative minor · 7. The major pentatonic as the major scale minus 4 and 7 | Play G major up and down in time with the metronome. Then improvise for 60 seconds over a G drone using only that scale. |
| **3** | **Intervals** _(was Level 2)_ | 1. Intervals as distances from the root, named by scale degree · 2. Shapes for the 3rd, 5th and octave, including the shift on the B string · 3. Major vs minor 3rd (the confusable pair) · 4. 4ths and 5ths, which give power chords · 5. 6ths and 7ths · 6. Hearing intervals in a key (drone, then find the degree) · 7. Mixed interval drill | The app plays a note over a drone. Find it on the fretboard and name its interval. Then play a short call-and-response phrase using only R, 3 and 5. |
| **4** | **Chords and CAGED** | 1. Building triads from 1-3-5 · 2. Major, minor and diminished triads · 3. The chords that fall on each degree of a major key · 4. Triads on three-string groups · 5. The C-A-G-E-D shapes as one movable chord · 6. Linking the five shapes up the neck · 7. 7th chords (maj7, 7, m7) | Play one chord, such as G, in all five CAGED shapes up the neck in time, and name the root note on each shape. |
| **5** | **Progressions** | 1. Roman numerals · 2. I–IV–V and the 12-bar blues · 3. I–V–vi–IV (the pop progression) · 4. ii–V–I · 5. Cadences and the pull of the V back to the I · 6. Recognizing progressions by ear · 7. Playing a progression in one CAGED position | Play a I–V–vi–IV in two keys with the backing track. Then pick out the progression of a song by ear (multiple choice from the audio). |
| **6** | **Improvising** | 1. The minor pentatonic in 5 positions, linked to CAGED · 2. Targeting chord tones on chord changes · 3. Phrasing: call and response, space, repetition · 4. Bends, slides and vibrato · 5. Blues and the b3/b7 sounds · 6. An introduction to modes (Dorian, Mixolydian) · 7. Soloing over a ii–V–I | Improvise a 12-bar blues chorus with the backing track and land on a chord tone at each chord change. Optionally record yourself and log it. |

**Throughout every level:** note-finding, interval and chord-spelling drills feed a spaced review deck. Once a level is finished, its drills mix into later review sessions. Every lesson ends with a quiz where you tap or play the answer.

### Three features that would set the app apart

1. **"Hear it, find it, play it" drills set in a key.** A drone or chord establishes the key, a note or interval sounds, the learner taps it on the fretboard, and the app explains why with the scale degree and its distance from the root. No app we reviewed combines ear training, fretboard position and theory explanations. The research on linking sound and movement supports this approach.
2. **Spaced review driven by weak spots, with a fretboard heat map.** Each fret, scale degree and interval is tracked for accuracy and speed. Weak items come back on a growing schedule, and confusable pairs are drilled together. This answers the complaint that drills are "repetitive and don't measure skill," and it uses the two principles with the strongest evidence: retrieval and spacing.
3. **A theory path that carries on past the beginner stage and never blocks you.** Levels 4–6 cover CAGED, chord-tone soloing and modes, which is where Yousician, Fender Play and Simply Guitar stop. Tapping is the main way to answer, and the microphone is optional and forgiving: it accepts any octave, has a "mark correct" override, and never locks progress. Pricing is honest. This addresses the two most common complaints in the reviews.
